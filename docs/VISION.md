# Product vision

Computers have always made us adapt to them. We learn how to type into different applications, where to find buttons, which shortcuts to remember and how to phrase things so that software understands us. Voice has promised to make this easier, but most voice interfaces still treat speech primarily as another way of entering text.

Kivi's vision is to make voice a universal layer between people and their computers.

Kivi should be available in two ways. It can work across the computer when a person deliberately invokes it, regardless of which application they are using. It can also work through the microphone experiences that people already use inside applications. The familiar way of speaking does not disappear. People get a choice: ordinary voice input when they want transcription, or Kivi when they want speech understood in context.

This distinction becomes especially important in India. People do not always communicate in one language. A single thought can naturally move between an Indian language and English, or contain code-mixed speech. Kivi should be designed around this reality rather than making people adapt themselves to rigid language settings. Sarvam's speech technology gives it the ability to work across 22 Indian languages and English, making multilingual speech a fundamental part of the experience.

Kivi should understand more than words. When someone is thinking aloud, Kivi can help organise the thought into something useful. When someone wants to write, Kivi can turn natural speech into appropriate language. This should work everywhere: documents, messages, coding environments and AI applications.

Writing should also work in the opposite direction. If words already exist on screen, the user should be able to select a sentence and speak the change they want. They could ask Kivi to shorten it, change its tone, simplify it, correct it or rewrite it for a different audience. The user should not have to copy the sentence into another AI tool and bring it back.

Kivi should also help people understand what comes into their computer. While reading an article, research paper, technical document or unfamiliar piece of code, users should be able to point at the relevant content and ask Kivi to explain it, clarify it or translate it. The same thought may also need to be expressed differently depending on who receives it, what language they speak and where the conversation is happening. Kivi should help preserve the user's meaning while adapting its expression.

As people use Kivi, it should learn the small things that make communication personal: names, terminology, preferences and corrections. This is where memory should strengthen every other part of the experience.

These capabilities can be expressed through five fundamental intentions:

- **Think** — organise what I am thinking.
- **Write** — create or change what I want to say.
- **Understand** — make sense of what I am receiving.
- **Communicate** — express what I mean appropriately to someone else.
- **Remember** — learn how I work.

The interaction underneath them should remain simple: Speak → Kivi understands → Kivi helps.

Understanding must not mean taking control away from the user. Kivi should preserve meaning, make important interpretations visible and keep the user in control of changes. It should not silently rewrite what someone meant or turn into an autonomous agent acting throughout the computer.

Kivi's ambition is not to replace Google, ChatGPT, Cursor, messaging apps or the applications people already use. It is to make all of them easier to interact with through language.

The long-term vision is a computer that does not require people to translate their thoughts into the language of software.
